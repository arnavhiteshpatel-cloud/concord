## Notes

**Internal consistency:** Internal-consistency coefficients (Cronbach alpha, McDonald omega) are only meaningful for a set of items that are intended to measure ONE construct (a reflective, unidimensional scale, e.g. the items of a questionnaire). They are NOT appropriate for an arbitrary set of task metrics (e.g. throughput, error rate, path efficiency, latency): such metrics are formative/heterogeneous, may legitimately be uncorrelated, and a low or high alpha says nothing about the reliability of any single metric. Use test-retest ICC for task metrics.

**Measurement invariance scope:** Formal measurement invariance (multi-group confirmatory factor analysis testing configural, metric/weak, scalar/strong and strict invariance; Meredith 1993; Vandenberg & Lance 2000) and differential item functioning are OUT OF SCOPE for v1 because: (1) NeuroMetric task scores are single observed metrics, not multiple reflective indicators of one latent factor, so a factor model per group is not identified or not meaningful; (2) multi-group CFA needs several hundred participants per group for stable estimates, far beyond the planned pilot samples; (3) no CFA/SEM engine is among the vetted dependencies. v1 therefore checks the observable consequences that matter for using a score across input modalities: equal reliability (ICC difference with bootstrap CI) and equivalent means within a pre-specified margin (TOST). Neither establishes that the score measures the same construct in each group; that requires construct-validity evidence per group.

**MCID:** MCID / MID cannot be computed by this software. A minimal clinically important difference is the smallest change that users, clinicians or caregivers judge to be important; establishing it requires ANCHOR-BASED evidence from real participants - e.g. a global rating of change, a patient-reported or clinician-rated functional anchor, or achievement of a meaningful real-world goal - linked to observed score changes (Revicki et al. 2008; de Vet & Terwee 2010) [PK]. It cannot be derived from simulated users, from the score distribution alone, or from reliability statistics: the minimal detectable change (MDC) says what change exceeds measurement noise (statistically detectable), not what change matters (clinically important). Distribution-based quantities (0.5 SD, 1 SEM, MDC) are reported only as 'distribution-based estimate, not an MCID' and may be used as supportive evidence to triangulate anchor-based estimates in a future participant study.

## Limitations

- All data are synthetic (simulated virtual participants).
- The task battery and derived scores are experimental and not validated as clinical outcome assessments.
- Browser timing: absolute times include unmeasured input and display latency; comparisons are valid within a fixed device/browser/input setup.
- Associations between levels of the measurement chain are not causal effects.
- No minimal clinically important difference is defined for any NeuroMetric score.
- Reliability estimates depend on the sample's between-person variance; they do not transfer to other populations automatically.
