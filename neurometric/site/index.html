Functional-outcome measurement and real-world evidence research

A device that works is not the same as a life that changes.

NeuroMetric is building neutral, input-agnostic research infrastructure to measure whether brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), assistive technology and neurorehabilitation produce meaningful improvements in everyday function, independence and care burden, and not only whether the device operates.

The scientific problem: device operation is not meaningful benefit

Neural interfaces and assistive input devices are usually judged by engineering measures: decoding accuracy, bits per second, cursor throughput, latency. These describe how well the device operates. They do not show whether a person can send a message to family, book an appointment, use a computer without help, or whether a care partner’s workload has changed.

The field recognises this gap:

  • A 2025 review of implantable motor BCI studies found that only 20 of 112 (17.9%) reported clinical outcomes; the literature is dominated by engineering outcomes (Dohle et al., Advanced Science, 2025).
  • FDA’s 2021 guidance on implanted BCI devices recommends a validated effectiveness endpoint, a minimal clinically important difference, and study of devices in realistic home-use settings. No BCI-specific clinical outcome assessment has been qualified through FDA’s Medical Device Development Tools programme.
  • A 2026 BCI Society workshop summary names “the lack of widely validated and fit-for-purpose” outcome assessments and “the need to capture meaningful outcomes in home and daily-life environments” as key challenges (Sawyer et al., Journal of Neural Engineering, 2026).
  • The implantable BCI Collaborative Community (iBCI-CC) stated in October 2026 that no single existing measure is likely to be sufficient, and called for a “shared measurement chain” from device performance to function to health outcome or care burden.

What engineering metrics tell you

  • How accurately and quickly selections are made
  • How much information per second the input carries
  • How the device behaves in a controlled session

What people, clinicians and payers also need to know

  • Can everyday digital tasks be completed, and how reliably?
  • Is the device used at home, and independently or with help?
  • Does care-partner assistance or burden change over time?

The measurement chain

Each link has to be measured, not assumed. NeuroMetric is designed to record observations at every level with their provenance, so that the relationships between levels can be studied rather than taken for granted.

  1. Device performance

    What does the input do?

    Selection accuracy, throughput, latency, false and missed activations, recorded from the same canonical event stream for every input method.

  2. Function

    Can the person complete digital tasks?

    A standardised battery: pointing, selecting, scrolling, dragging, typing, and simulated everyday activities such as messaging or booking an appointment.

  3. Everyday independence

    Is it used at home, and without help?

    Consented, aggregate home-use summaries, and whether tasks were completed independently, with assistance, with another input, or not at all.

  4. Care burden and participation

    Does the help people need change?

    Structured capture of care-partner assistance (setup, physical, verbal, troubleshooting, calibration, task completion) and licensed questionnaires where permitted.

  5. Evidence

    Can others check it?

    Raw events stored verbatim with checksums, scoring that can be recomputed, audit trails, and pre-specified analyses packaged for research and regulatory discussion.

What the chain does not do

Associations between levels are analysed separately within and between people, because stable differences (ability, care setting) confound simple correlations. Nothing in NeuroMetric produces a causal or economic claim automatically, and any composite “independence” score remains labelled experimental until it is validated against external evidence.

Who it is meant to serve

NeuroMetric is intended to be neutral: the same tasks and records for any input method, so that results are comparable and are not produced by the maker of the device being evaluated.

People who use the devices, and their care partners

Their priorities should define what counts as meaningful. Measurement should reflect daily life, record help honestly (assistance is not failure), and never collect more than is needed.

Researchers

Academic BCI and assistive-technology groups who need comparable functional measures across devices, with open metric definitions and reproducible scoring.

Assistive-technology clinicians

Clinicians and rehabilitation researchers comparing access methods (switches, eye gaze, head pointers, BCIs) on the same tasks, with timing reported honestly for each setup.

Device developers and study sponsors

Teams that need neutral, home-use functional evidence for BCIs, communication aids and home neurorehabilitation. NeuroMetric has no sponsors or partners today.

What NeuroMetric is not

  • Not a decoder, and it does not process neural signals.
  • Not a device controller: it never sends commands to a BCI, stimulator or other device.
  • Not a neural acquisition SDK.
  • Not a validated clinical outcome assessment (yet), and it makes no diagnostic or treatment claims.
  • Not a regulatory submission, and it has no regulatory clearance.
