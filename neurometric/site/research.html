Research

Testing the measurements before testing with people

The current work is computational: it asks whether NeuroMetric’s metrics behave as good measurements should under controlled, simulated conditions, and whether the software processes real recorded movement data correctly.

Limitations: read these first

Simulation is not clinical evidence. Every result on this site comes from synthetic virtual users, software verification, or non-clinical public data. None comes from people who use BCIs or assistive technology.

  • No human participants. Reliability, validity and responsiveness in people have not been measured. That requires an IRB-approved study.
  • No physiology. Simulated noise is not derived from neural tuning, spike counts or electrode properties. The simulator models no specific device.
  • Simplified users. Each modality uses one control law. Real people switch strategies, slow down near targets, learn a decoder’s quirks and adapt across sessions; the model does not, except through explicit parameter changes.
  • Selection is modelled as a separate channel. Real click decoders may couple clicking with movement.
  • Assumed population structure. Simulated reliability depends on an assumed latent-ability structure; real between-person variation may be larger or differently shaped.
  • Modality models are partial. The gaze model ignores blinks, head movement and calibration; the scanning model ignores anticipation strategies used by experienced switch users.
  • No hardware testing. Browser input adapters have been exercised with synthetic input only.
  • No clinically important change. NeuroMetric does not compute MCIDs; that requires anchor-based clinical evidence.
  • Calibration targets are approximate. Simulator profiles were tuned to sit within broad published ranges recalled from the literature; these ranges still need to be checked against primary sources before any publication.

What simulation can show: that a metric responds in the expected direction and with adequate sensitivity under the defined conditions, and that analysis code recovers known truth. What it cannot show: that the metric is reliable, valid or meaningful for any person.

The computational programme

Degradation sweeps

Each sweep worsens one property of the simulated input in steps (noise, latency, dropouts, calibration drift, false activations, missed selections, directional bias, update rate, gain) and runs the same virtual participants through the battery at every level.

Measurement properties

Simulated test–retest reliability versus test length, responsiveness to a known simulated improvement, comparability across simulated modalities, device-versus-user variance, the effect of incomplete sessions, and fixed versus adaptive difficulty.

Software verification

Monte-Carlo checks of the statistical estimators (bias, error and confidence-interval coverage under known truth), bit-identical reproducibility across processes, and event-processing throughput.

Real public data

Reprocessing of an openly licensed movement dataset to check the event pipeline and metrics on real recorded trajectories. The dataset used so far is a non-human primate hand-control recording: not BCI control and not a clinical population.

How a degradation sweep is summarised

Each metric is first oriented so that higher means better. Across the levels of a sweep we then report:

Spearman ρ
Rank correlation between the score and the degradation level. Expected to be negative.
Monotone fraction
Share of adjacent levels whose mean moves in the expected direction.
First-step effect (d)
Standardised paired difference between the baseline and the first degraded level, for the same virtual participants.
Minimum detectable level
The lowest level at which the effect versus baseline reaches |d| ≥ 0.5.
Adjacent-level AUC
Mean probability that a score at one level beats a score at the next, worse level (0.5 means no discrimination).
Undefined, floor, ceiling
Share of scores that are undefined (for example no successful trials) or sit at the natural bound of the metric.
Simulated retest ICC
Agreement between two repetitions with the same virtual participants. This reflects the simulator’s assumptions, not human reliability.

Metric definitions (v1)

Raw measurements are recorded directly (sample positions and times, selections, keystrokes, markers, assistance events). Derived metrics are computed from them by published formulas, and every one can be recomputed from the stored event stream.

Observable-only rule. Metric functions take only data a real deployment can record. Simulation ground truth (intended selections, injected false activations, dropouts) is stored separately and used only to check the metrics.

Simulation methods in brief

The simulation is closed-loop with delayed perception: virtual users see the degraded cursor after a feedback delay and correct for it, as people do. A purely open-loop noise model would overstate the harm of bias and drift, which users partly compensate, and understate the harm of latency, which destabilises feedback control. This follows feedback-control models of BCI users used in decoder design (Willett et al., 2019).

Virtual-user models include a proportional feedback controller with signal-dependent noise (velocity control), minimum-jerk submovements with corrective submovements (position control), a saccade-and-fixation gaze model with dwell, and row–column or linear switch scanning with log-normal reaction times. Populations share one latent ability that scales speed and noise, plus individual variation; fatigue and learning are explicit, labelled parameter changes.

Each degradation parameter is checked by a unit test against its analytic expectation, and every simulated record carries a synthetic flag. Runs are seeded and reproducible.
