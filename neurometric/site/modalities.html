Modalities

One battery, many ways to operate a computer

The battery accepts any input the operating system presents as a pointer, a key or a switch. The modality is declared at setup and stamped on every recorded event, so results from different inputs stay distinguishable.

Testing status for every modality below

Browser-tested with synthetic input only. No modality has yet been tested with real assistive-technology hardware, with a BCI, or with participants. Support means “the software is designed to accept and record this input”, not “this has been shown to work well for people who use it”.

Why the operating system is the boundary

A web page cannot tell whether a pointer movement came from a mouse, an eye tracker, a head pointer or a BCI exposed as a mouse, or whether a Space key press came from a keyboard or a switch interface. NeuroMetric therefore asks for the modality at setup and records the browser’s observed pointer type separately in session metadata.

This is a deliberate design choice. NeuroMetric works with the input the person already uses, through the access settings they already have, and never needs privileged access to a device. The cost is that it cannot see inside the device: decoder confidence, signal quality and similar values are only available if an authorised source supplies them, and are then stored as device metadata rather than recomputed.

Some platforms now offer BCI-specific routes. Apple, for example, documents a BCI Human Interface Device protocol that lets a BCI act as a switch for Switch Control on iOS, iPadOS and visionOS 26 and later. It is a firmware-side specification for device makers; we found no documented way for third-party apps to read BCI reports. From NeuroMetric’s side, such a device still appears as a switch.

Timing, honestly

  • For switch scanning, dwell, eye gaze, head pointer and BCI input, the measured interval includes scan rate, dwell time or decoder latency. It is reported as selection time, never as reaction time, and these settings are stored with the session.
  • Hardware-validated studies find browser-recorded response times run tens of milliseconds late on average, with variability that depends on platform, browser and keyboard. NeuroMetric therefore makes within-participant, same-device, same-browser, same-modality comparisons, and does not claim millisecond-accurate absolute times.
  • Comparing raw times across people who used different modalities, devices or browsers requires modelling those factors.

Modalities in simulation

Separately from the browser, the simulated-decoder harness models mouse-like, BCI-cursor-like, gaze-with-dwell and switch-scanning control with synthetic virtual users. These models exist to stress-test the metrics. They are not measurements of any device and are calibrated only to sit inside broad published ranges.

What would change the status above

  1. Bench testing with real switch interfaces, an eye tracker and a head pointer.
  2. Usability review by assistive-technology clinicians.
  3. An IRB-approved pilot with participants who use these inputs, run with a faculty principal investigator.
  4. For BCIs, a collaboration with a research programme or device developer under an appropriate agreement.
