## Key information (please read first)
- This is a research study. Taking part is **voluntary**. You can stop at any time without
  penalty.
- We are testing new, experimental computer tasks: pointing, selecting, scrolling, dragging,
  copy-typing short phrases, and practice activities such as finding a fictional contact.

## Why is this study being done?
To find out whether a new set of computer tasks works as designed, whether it can tell apart
different ways of using a computer, and whether people score similarly on different days. The
tasks are not a medical test, and your scores are not interpreted as health information.

## What will happen?
- A researcher sets up a laptop, screen and mouse for you.
- You answer a few questions: age range, sex, which hand you use, and how often you use a
  computer.
- You complete computer tasks after a short practice for each. You rate your tiredness from 0 to
  10 at several points and fill in short questionnaires about effort (NASA-TLX) and ease of use
  (SUS).
- *[Stage 2 only]* You try four ways of controlling the computer: a mouse, a head-tracking
  pointer using a webcam, an eye-tracker pointer, and a single button with automatic scanning.
  The order is assigned by chance. The webcam and eye tracker only move the pointer. **We do not
  record or save video, images or where you look.**
- *[Stage 3 only]* You come back on four more days, 2–7 days apart, at about the same time of
  day.
- You may take breaks at any time. We will also ask you to take short breaks.

## What information is collected?
**Collected:**
- the timing and position of pointer movements, selections and key presses **within the study
  tasks**;
- your task scores;
- the questionnaire answers listed above;
- technical information about the computer and browser;
- notes if a researcher helped you.

**Not collected:**
- anything outside the study tasks;
- audio, video or photos of you;
- eye-gaze coordinates;
- your own text. You copy the study's phrases only.

## Risks and discomforts
- Tiredness, frustration, mild eye or neck strain (especially with the head or eye pointer).
- A small risk of a confidentiality breach. We reduce it by using a code number instead of your
  name on study data, keeping your name and the code key separately in a secure university
  location, and limiting who can see the data.

## Benefits
None directly. The results may help develop better ways to measure computer access for people
with disabilities.
